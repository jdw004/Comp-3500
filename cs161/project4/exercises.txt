1. Explain how to avoid starvation in your implementation.

Starvation is avoided in my implementation by using a turn based system managed by the shared variable turnFlag. 
Only one species is allowed to eat at a time and when cats or mice arrive, they must wait on their own semaphore until it becomes their turn. 
At the start of each turn, only a limited number of a species can eat, matching the number of available bowls. 
When the last animal of that group finishes eating, the program checks if the other species is waiting and switches the turn to them. 
If no animals are waiting, the turn resets so that whichever species arrives next can take it. 
This approach ensures that cats and mice alternate fairly, and no group can continuously occupy the bowls, which prevents starvation.

2. Comment on your experience of implementing the Cats-and-Mice program. Can you 
derive any principles about the use of the semaphore synchronization primitives?

Implementing the Cats and Mice program helped me understand how semaphores can be used to control access to shared resources and ensure proper synchronization between concurrent threads to prevent deadlocks. 
I learned that semaphores are powerful tools for ensuring mutual exclusion and coordinating the order in which threads run. 
The program showed the importance of protecting shared variables with a mutex and using separate semaphores to manage waiting groups. 
It also demonstrated that synchronization requires careful planning to avoid issues like deadlock and starvation. 
Overall, the main principle I derived is that semaphores should be used to balance fairness and efficiency, allowing multiple threads to cooperate safely without interfering with each other.
